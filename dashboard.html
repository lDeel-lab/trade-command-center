📖 How every recommendation is computed — the full logic, nothing hidden

The philosophy — spot-first, survive-first

This system is built around four beliefs. 1) Spot first: you buy the real asset and hold it — no leverage that can liquidate you, no futures expiry, no funding fees bleeding you. Being wrong then costs time, not everything. 2) Liquid assets only: nothing enters the universe unless it trades on a major venue (IG / Bybit) with real volume — an illiquid gem with a great story is still a trap, because a handful of players can move it and you can't get out at a fair price. That rule is exactly how the 🌍 Emerging tab works: Brazil, Mexico, Chile, Argentina, South Africa, Indonesia, Kazakhstan and more — but always through their deeply-traded NYSE/NASDAQ listings (ADRs), never a thin local exchange, so every name is reachable through IG, Interactive Brokers or Revolut with real liquidity behind it. 3) Capital preservation beats profit chasing: every plan starts from "what does being wrong cost?", positions are sized from that, and assets that can realistically go to zero are flagged so a buy-and-hold can survive a drawdown. 4) Watch/wait is a real answer: most days the honest output is "nothing meets the bar — here's what we're watching". A system that always finds you a trade is selling you something. This one is allowed to say ⏳.

Step 1 — Score each timeframe (−5 … +5), from de-correlated blocks

For every instrument on every timeframe the score is built from five independent evidence blocks, so five different views of the same uptrend can't stack into false confidence: trend structure ±2 (the swing sequence — HH/HL vs LH/LL); MA alignment ±1 (price vs 50EMA vs 200EMA, all in order or not); momentum ±1 (RSI and MACD histogram collapsed into ONE block, because they measure the same thing); volatility trigger ±1 (a Bollinger squeeze that just fired, in its direction); volume confirmation ±0.5 (a >2σ anomaly agreeing with the candle). Each timeframe also carries a regime tag: directional efficiency (how straight price travelled vs how far it wandered) ≥0.3 = trending, below = ranging, plus an ATR percentile for volatility state. Trend strategies are hard-gated to trending regimes and fades to ranging ones — the right tool is only offered in the right market.

Step 2 — Combine timeframe groups → trade type & holding period

Timeframes form three groups: LTF (5m/15m/30m), MTF (1H/4H), HTF (D/W/Q). The combination decides everything: all three agree → 🎯 ALIGNED (hold days→weeks); MTF+HTF agree while LTF pulls against → 🌊 SWING (2–10 days — the LTF dip IS the entry); LTF+MTF agree without HTF backing → ⚡ INTRADAY (hours–1 day, conviction capped at 65 because it fights the bigger trend); only HTF strong → 🗓 POSITION (2–8 weeks, building); anything conflicting → MIXED, never shown as a trade.

Step 3 — Direction, Entry, and Grade (three separate questions)

Direction conviction (0–100) starts from the strength of the group agreement, then adjusts: a strategy trigger agreeing with the profile +8; fundamentals score (supply math for crypto, valuation/growth/quality for stocks) adds up to ±9 — asymmetric, since weak fundamentals help a short; extension guard: price stretched >2.2×ATR beyond the 50EMA in the trade direction −6, >3×ATR −12. Entry quality (0–100) is scored separately — "is HERE a good place to enter?": stretch past the mean is penalised, price pulled back near the 20EMA or into an unfilled FVG is rewarded, taut rubber-bands (p90+ MA-distance percentiles) and RSI extremes are docked. The two combine into a grade: A+ (rare — strong direction AND clean entry), A (alert-worthy), B, C, or ⏳ WAIT — entry quality below 40 is a hard WAIT no matter how good the direction: right trade, wrong moment, and the card tells you to wait for the pullback instead of chasing. Only A+ and A picks are sent to Telegram. That's the fix for the all-time-high problem — "direction good" no longer means "buy now".

Step 4 — The trade plan

Entry prefers a pullback into an unfilled fair value gap behind price (within 2 ATR); otherwise at market ±0.25 ATR. Stop goes beyond the liquidity pool (clustered equal highs/lows) plus a 0.35 ATR buffer — outside the stop-hunt zone — falling back to 1.5×ATR when no clean pool exists. TP1/TP2/TP3 are structural targets 🧱: candidate levels are collected from real map features — opposing liquidity pools, prior swings, value-area edges and the volume-profile POC — and the nearest sensible ones become the targets (TP1 ≈ 0.6–1.8R, TP2 and TP3 further out), falling back to 1R/2R/3R only when no clean structure exists. The market doesn't owe anyone round R-multiples; it does respect the levels where orders actually sit. The suggested management: a third off at each target, stop to break-even after TP1. Position size always derives from the stop so a full stop-out costs ≈ your chosen risk % (gaps and slippage can make it modestly worse, especially over weekends and news) — being wrong is designed to cost one small loss, never "getting stuck for ages". If the stop is hit, the trade is over.

The rubber-band meter (MA distance)

Every instrument's detail view shows its distance from EMA 9, EMA 21, SMA 50, SMA 100 and SMA 200 — in %, in ATR multiples (so volatile and quiet assets are comparable), and as a historical percentile: how rare today's stretch is versus that instrument's own last 200 bars. p90+ in the trade's direction means the band is taut and mean-reversion risk is elevated — the case text warns you, and the extension guard has already docked conviction. Percentile measures rarity, not destiny: strong trends can stay extended.

The Rotation view — "there is always a bull market somewhere"

Every market group (crypto, metals, energy, agriculture, each equity region, FX) is scored by volatility-adjusted momentum — a blend of 1-, 3- and 6-month returns divided by daily ATR, so wild and quiet assets rank fairly — plus the share of members above their 200-day MA. Groups ranking 🐂 BULL are where the money currently is; the Focus list combines group strength with each instrument's own relative strength to surface the few leaders worth your attention. Trade the leader of the leading group; ignore the laggards; re-rank weekly, because rotation is measured in weeks and months.

The AI reviewer (optional)

When an Anthropic API key is configured, every new ≥70 signal is independently reviewed by Claude before it is alerted: it reads the technicals, fundamentals and tagged headlines (and can run a live web search for fresh news) and verdicts the trade — ✅ CONFIRM, ⚠️ CAUTION with named risks, or ⛔ VETO. The verdict is advisory only: a veto is shown inside the alert but never suppresses it, because an unproven filter must first earn its authority — the Performance tab scores every verdict against real outcomes, and only if vetoes demonstrably save money should they ever be given the kill switch. The reviewer's job is to find reasons the signal is wrong — imminent earnings, contradicting news, stale-data artifacts — not to agree with it.

What this system does NOT know — read this honestly

It is a momentum/structure system: it will always lean with the trend, which means it will sometimes flag strength near tops — the extension guard and the against-the-trade section are the counterweights, not a cure. Conviction measures how strongly the evidence aligns, not a verified win-rate (that calibrates over weeks of recorded picks). It doesn't know earnings dates, insider flows, or idiosyncratic company risk beyond the fundamentals score and tagged news. And nothing here is financial advice — it is a systematic second opinion that always tells you where it's wrong (the invalidation level) before you enter.

📚 Knowledge base — the big-picture stuff, in plain English

This is your reference shelf. Nothing here changes by the minute — it's the background physics of markets. When a headline says "CPI came in hot" or "the yield curve un-inverted", come here, find the term, and you'll know whether that's a tailwind or a headwind for what you hold.

💵 Money supply & M2 — how much money exists

M2 is simply a count of all the easily-spendable money in the economy: physical cash, current accounts, savings accounts, and money-market funds. Think of it as the amount of water in the pool. When central banks print money or make borrowing cheap, M2 grows — more money is sloshing around looking for somewhere to go, and some of it always finds its way into stocks, crypto and property. When M2 shrinks or flatlines, there is less spare money to push prices up.
Bullish when: M2 is growing again after a squeeze — historically great for Bitcoin and growth stocks.
Bearish when: M2 is contracting (money being drained) — risk assets fight an uphill battle.
Rule of thumb: Bitcoin, more than almost anything, is a bet on the pool getting fuller.

🛒 CPI — the inflation number everyone waits for

CPI (Consumer Price Index) measures how much prices are rising for ordinary shoppers — food, rent, petrol, services. Released monthly, and it moves markets within seconds. Why it matters: central banks raise interest rates to fight high inflation, and higher rates hurt stocks and crypto. So the market's reaction is really about what CPI means for rates.
Bullish when: CPI comes in lower than expected ("cooling inflation") — rate cuts get closer, risk assets rally.
Bearish when: CPI comes in hotter than expected — rate cuts get pushed out, dollar jumps, stocks and crypto sell off.
Watch "Core CPI" too — the same measure with food and energy stripped out (they're volatile). Central banks trust Core more than the headline number.

🏭 PPI — inflation upstream, in the factories

PPI (Producer Price Index) measures the prices businesses pay each other — raw materials, components, wholesale goods. It's the same story as CPI but earlier in the pipeline: if factories are paying more today, shops charge more in a few months. Traders use PPI as a preview of where CPI is heading.
Bullish when: PPI is falling — the inflation pipeline is emptying, future CPI likely cooler.
Bearish when: PPI is surging — inflation is being cooked upstream and will show in CPI later.

🏦 Interest rates — the price of money, and the master switch

When the Fed (US), Bank of England or ECB moves rates, they change the price of borrowing for the entire world. Rates up = loans and mortgages cost more, companies invest less, and — crucially — boring savings accounts start paying decent interest, so money leaves risky assets. Rates down = borrowing is cheap, savings pay nothing, and money is pushed out along the risk curve into stocks and crypto.
Bullish: rate cuts, or even just hints of cuts ("dovish" talk).
Bearish: rate hikes or "higher for longer" talk ("hawkish").
Nuance: the first cut sometimes comes because something broke — cuts during a panic are not the same as cuts during a soft landing. Ask why they're cutting.

📈 Bonds & yields — the "boring" market that bosses all the others

A government bond is an IOU: lend the government money, get interest. The yield is the interest rate that IOU effectively pays. Key mechanics: when bond prices fall, yields rise, and vice versa — they're two ends of the same seesaw. Why you care: the US 10-year yield is the world's benchmark "safe return". When it rises, every risky asset must compete with a better safe alternative.
Bullish for stocks/crypto: yields falling (bonds rallying) for calm reasons — inflation cooling.
Bearish for stocks/crypto: yields rising fast — especially bad for tech/growth stocks and crypto, whose promised profits sit far in the future and get discounted harder.
Panic exception: when yields collapse very fast, it can mean recession fear — everything sells off together first.

↕️ The yield curve — the market's recession radar

Normally, lending for 10 years pays more than lending for 2 (you're locked in longer — you demand more). Plot yields from short to long and you get an upward-sloping yield curve. When short-term yields rise above long-term ones, the curve is inverted — the market is saying "rates are painfully high now, but they'll be cut later because the economy will weaken." An inverted curve has preceded most US recessions, usually with a long delay (12–24 months).
The sneaky part: the danger moment is often not the inversion itself but the rapid un-inversion ("steepening") as the recession actually arrives and cuts begin.
Plain rule: inverted curve = late-cycle caution; fast re-steepening after inversion = be defensive; a normal, gently sloping curve = healthy backdrop.

💲 DXY — the dollar index, the seesaw under everything

DXY measures the US dollar against a basket of other major currencies (mostly the euro). Nearly everything you trade is priced in dollars, so the dollar is the ruler you measure with — when the ruler grows, everything measured by it looks smaller.
Dollar UP = headwind for gold, oil, copper, Bitcoin, emerging markets, and US companies selling abroad.
Dollar DOWN = tailwind for all of the above — commodities and crypto love a weak dollar.
Why it moves: mostly interest-rate differences (higher US rates attract money into dollars) and safe-haven flows (crises make people hoard dollars).

🥇 Gold & real yields

Gold pays no interest. So its main competitor is the real yield — what bonds pay after subtracting inflation. When real yields are high, holding gold costs you the interest you gave up. When real yields are low or negative, gold shines.
Bullish gold: falling real yields, weakening dollar, war/crisis fear, central banks buying.
Bearish gold: rising real yields and a strong dollar.
Bitcoin increasingly trades on the same logic — "digital gold" — but with far bigger swings and a heavier dose of pure risk appetite.

🖨 QE & QT — the liquidity tide

QE (quantitative easing): the central bank creates money and buys bonds with it, flooding the system with cash — the tide comes in, and almost all boats rise. QT (quantitative tightening): the reverse — bonds roll off, money is drained, the tide goes out.
Bullish: QE starting, QT ending or slowing.
Bearish: QT running at full pace.
Many big rallies and busts line up better with this liquidity tide than with earnings or news. It's the slow-moving force underneath the daily noise — check it monthly, not hourly.

😱 VIX — the fear gauge

VIX measures how much protection (options) on the S&P 500 costs — effectively, how scared big money is. Under ~15: calm. 15–25: normal worry. Above 30: fear. Above 40: panic.
For a buyer: a VIX spike above 35–40 has historically been closer to a buying opportunity than a selling one — panic marks bottoms more often than tops. A VIX that's been pinned very low for months is the opposite warning: everyone's relaxed, nobody's braced for a shock.

🚦 Risk-on / risk-off — which mood is the market in?

Risk-ON: money flows into stocks, crypto, emerging markets, copper, high-beta names; the dollar and gold drift down. Risk-OFF: money runs to dollars, US bonds, gold, and the Swiss franc / Japanese yen; everything risky sells off together. Knowing the current mood matters because in risk-off, correlations go to one — your "diversified" holdings all fall at once. That's why this dashboard's rotation tab and correlation guard exist.

👷 Jobs data — NFP & unemployment

NFP (Non-Farm Payrolls), first Friday of each month, counts new US jobs. It's read through the rates lens, so the logic can feel upside-down:
Very strong jobs = economy hot = rates stay high = often bearish for stocks in a fighting-inflation era.
Modestly cooling jobs = rate cuts closer = often bullish.
Collapsing jobs = recession = bearish, cuts or no cuts. Good news can be bad news, and bad news good — until it's really bad news.

📊 Company numbers — EPS, P/E and forward P/E

EPS (earnings per share): company profit divided by number of shares — profit per slice. P/E (price-to-earnings): share price ÷ EPS — how many years of today's profit you're paying for. A P/E of 15 is average-ish; 30+ means the market expects strong growth; under 10 means cheap or troubled — find out which. Forward P/E uses next year's expected profit instead of last year's — better for fast-growing companies, but it's built on forecasts that can be wrong.
The trap: a low P/E is not automatically a bargain (dying businesses look cheap all the way down), and a high P/E is not automatically a bubble (great growers can grow into it). P/E is a question, not an answer.

₿ Crypto-specific forces

The halving: roughly every 4 years, the new supply of Bitcoin per day is cut in half. Less new supply with the same demand has historically preceded the big bull cycles (with a lag of months). ETF flows: since spot ETFs launched, daily inflows/outflows are a direct read on institutional demand — sustained inflows are structurally bullish. Stablecoin supply (USDT+USDC total): this is the cash sitting on the sidelines inside crypto — growing supply = dry powder building. Funding rates: what leveraged futures traders pay to hold positions. Heavily positive funding = crowd is leveraged long = vulnerable to a flush. As a spot holder you don't pay funding — but extreme funding warns you when a violent shakeout is likely. Shakeouts are when spot buyers get their prices.

💧 Liquidity, spreads, float — why we're picky about what's tradeable

Liquidity is how much you can buy or sell without moving the price. Spread is the gap between the buy and sell price — a hidden fee you pay twice. Float is how many shares/coins actually circulate freely. Thin liquidity + small float = a market a few players can shove around — pumps, dumps, and 30% wicks. That's why this system's rule is: no matter how exciting the story, an illiquid asset never becomes a trade. Liquid majors can still fall hard — but they can't easily be faked, and you can always get out at a fair price.
